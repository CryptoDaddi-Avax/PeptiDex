What it does

Oxytocin is an endogenous nine-amino-acid hormone produced in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior pituitary. It is central to childbirth and lactation, but research interest centers on its role in the brain.

Intranasal oxytocin is among the most-studied neuropeptides for social cognition, trust, and anxiety, alongside peripheral effects on smooth muscle and gut motility.

Why researchers study it

  • Most-studied neuropeptide for social behavior and bonding
  • Reduced amygdala reactivity to social stress in imaging studies
  • Anxiolytic and pro-affiliative effects
  • Peripheral effects on smooth muscle and gut motility

Mechanism

Oxytocin activates the oxytocin receptor (OXTR), a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in the amygdala, hypothalamus, and reward circuitry as well as in uterine and mammary smooth muscle.

Central OXTR activation modulates social-salience and fear circuits — notably dampening amygdala reactivity — while peripheral activation drives uterine contraction, milk let-down, and gut motility.

Social cognition

Central OXTR activation modulates trust, bonding, and social salience.

Anxiety reduction

Dampens amygdala reactivity to social and emotional stress.

Smooth-muscle effects

Drives uterine contraction, milk let-down, and gut motility peripherally.
