## What is GHK-Cu?

GHK-Cu is a copper complex of the tripeptide **glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine** (Gly-
His-Lys). The peptide occurs endogenously in human plasma — circulating
concentrations are reported to decline substantially after the third decade of
life — and has been studied since the 1970s for its effect on tissue remodelling
and wound repair [1].

Unlike most peptides covered on PeptiDex, GHK-Cu is most commonly administered
**topically** in cosmetic formulations rather than by injection, with a smaller
literature on subcutaneous and intralesional use in animal wound-healing models.

GHK-Cu is **not approved by the FDA** as a systemic therapeutic. Topical
cosmetic use of GHK-Cu in skin-care products is well-established and not subject
to drug-approval pathways.

## How GHK-Cu Works (Mechanism of Action)

### Copper delivery and pathway modulation

GHK is a high-affinity copper-binding tripeptide. The copper-bound complex
appears to be the bioactive species in most assays, and the published mechanism
literature proposes that GHK-Cu acts through **multiple complementary pathways**
[1][2]:

- Copper delivery for collagen and elastin biosynthesis (lysyl oxidase-mediated
  cross-linking).
- Stimulation of dermal fibroblast and keratinocyte activity, promoting
  re-epithelialisation [3].
- Modulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP1, MMP2) and TIMP1 to support
  balanced tissue remodelling [4].
- Anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, including reduced reactive-oxygen
  species and pro-inflammatory cytokine signalling [5][6].
- Broad transcriptional modulation: a 2014 bioinformatic analysis by Pickart et
  al. reported GHK-Cu can up- or down-regulate more than 4,000 human genes,
  shifting expression patterns from disease-associated to regenerative profiles
  [2].

## Clinical & Preclinical Evidence

### Human and clinical studies

The clinical literature on GHK-Cu is weighted heavily toward **topical cosmetic
use** rather than injectable systemic use. Pickart's 2008 review summarises
evidence for improvements in skin density, thickness, and visible signs of
photoaging from topical formulations [1].

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1. What is GHK-Cu?**
GHK-Cu is the copper complex of the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, a
naturally-occurring molecule in human plasma whose levels decline with age. It
has been studied for skin regeneration, wound healing, and broad gene-expression
modulation [1][2].

**Q2. Is GHK-Cu the same as topical "copper peptide" skin-care products?**
Most over-the-counter "copper peptide" skin-care products contain GHK-Cu (often
at 0.05–2% by weight) as the active ingredient. The injectable research-chemical
form is the same molecule used in topical cosmetic formulations.

**Q3. Does GHK-Cu actually stimulate collagen production?**
In-vitro work has reported that GHK-Cu increases collagen I/III gene expression
and regulates matrix metalloproteinases (MMP1, MMP2) and TIMP1 to support
balanced tissue remodelling [4]. Topical clinical formulations have reported
visible improvements in skin density and thickness [1].

**Q4. What is the half-life of GHK-Cu?**
The half-life of injected GHK-Cu has not been definitively published in
indexed literature.

**Q5. Can GHK-Cu be used topically?**
Yes — topical use in cosmetic formulations at 0.05–2% by weight is the most
common form of GHK-Cu administration and is supported by the longest body of
clinical literature [1][3].

**Q6. Does GHK-Cu modulate gene expression?**
A 2014 bioinformatic analysis by Pickart et al. reported GHK-Cu can up- or
down-regulate the expression of more than 4,000 human genes, shifting expression
patterns toward regenerative and antioxidant profiles [2]. This is a
transcriptomic-modelling result rather than a clinical outcome.

**Q7. Does GHK-Cu help with hair growth?**
Hair-growth claims for GHK-Cu are common in cosmetic marketing but the
peer-reviewed evidence is more limited than the skin-quality evidence.

**Q8. What animal models support GHK-Cu's anti-inflammatory effects?**
Recent preclinical work includes a zebrafish larval inflammation model showing
reduced neutrophil/macrophage migration and pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression
[6], and a mouse colitis model showing reduced inflammation via the SIRT1/STAT3
pathway [7]. These are early-phase mechanistic studies, not clinical evidence.

**Q9. Can GHK-Cu be combined with BPC-157 and TB-500?**
The on-site dataset records this triple combination as part of skin and tissue
repair stacks; no formal combination trial has been published. Use the
[interaction checker](/tools/interactions).

**Q10. Is GHK-Cu safe in pregnancy?**
Systemic GHK-Cu has not been studied in pregnancy; conservative exclusion is
appropriate. Topical cosmetic use of GHK-Cu falls under general cosmetic-safety
guidance.

**Q11. Does GHK-Cu protect against UV damage?**
In-vitro UV-exposure studies report photoprotective and antioxidant effects from
GHK-Cu, with activation of multiple antioxidant and tissue-repair genes [5].
Whether topical application produces meaningful in-vivo photoprotection is a
separate question that requires clinical photoprotection studies.

**Q12. Why is the half-life information so vague?**
Because most clinical work on GHK-Cu has used **topical** rather than systemic
administration, the systemic pharmacokinetics are less well characterised than
for injected research-only peptides. The 4,000-gene transcriptomic finding [2]
also illustrates that the molecule's bioactivity is more about pathway
modulation than a clean dose-response curve.
